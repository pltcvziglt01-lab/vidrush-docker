LIVELINESS — every shot must move, react and breathe. A flat, static shot is
a failed shot.

CAMERA: the camera is never locked off for a whole shot. Over eight seconds it
does at least two of these, slowly and smoothly:
- a slow push in toward a character as the beat builds
- a slow pull back to reveal the room or the distance between them
- a gentle drift sideways following a character as they move
It never shakes, never whip-pans and never spins. On the emotional beat of the
shot it pushes in close on the reacting character's face.

CARTOON EFFECTS — drawn in the same flat 2D style as the characters, with the
same bold black outlines. Use the ones that fit the beat, never more than two
at a time:
- short curved motion lines trailing a moving hand, head or body
- a small puff of white dust where feet land or where someone sits down hard
- one large sweat drop at the side of the head for stress or awkwardness
- small white steam puffs from the ears for anger
- a small cross-shaped anger vein mark on the forehead
- a few floating hearts for affection
- a tiny dark rain cloud hovering above the head for disappointment
- a burst of thin white radiating lines behind the head for shock or realisation
- small yellow four-pointed sparkles for delight or pride
- a slow wobble of the whole body for exhaustion or defeat
The room stays photorealistic; only the characters and their effects are flat
2D cartoon.

FACE: the face carries the joke. Eyes go wide, squeeze shut, roll, droop or
narrow. Eyebrows move on every beat. The mouth opens fully when reacting. Use
squash-and-stretch on the head, not just the body.

BEAT RHYTHM: something visible must change every two to three seconds — a
camera move, a pose change, an effect appearing, the light shifting. In an
eight second shot there are at least three such changes.

AMBIENCE: the real room sound of the scene, quiet and continuous — a clock,
distant traffic, a fridge hum, birds outside. No music.

RHYTHM — the single most important rule. A shot at one constant speed is dead.

SHOW, NEVER REFERENCE: if a line mentions "the third one", the viewer must have
watched the first and the second happen on screen. Never build a joke on
something that happened off-frame. If it matters, it is shown.

BURST AND HOLD: every shot contains at least one tempo change.
- BURST: three fast events inside two to three seconds, each with its own sharp
  sound and its own motion lines. The events escalate — faster, closer, louder.
- HOLD: immediately after the burst, one and a half to two seconds of almost
  total stillness. The character does not move at all. The ambience drops to
  just a ticking clock. This silence is the laugh; it only works because the
  burst came first.
- The deadpan line to camera comes AFTER the hold, never during the burst.

SPEED IS A CHARACTER TRAIT: BIBI's world is fast — doors banging, things flying,
quick cuts of motion. BOBO's world is slow — he barely moves. The comedy is the
collision of those two speeds inside one frame, so never let them share a tempo.
EDITING AND RHYTHM — applies to every comedy clip. Read this before writing any
shot. A clip that only sets something up is a failed clip.

EVERY CLIP IS ITS OWN JOKE: each eight second clip must have a beginning, a
middle and a small payoff of its own. It may set up the next clip as well, but
it can never be pure setup. If a viewer watched only this one clip, they must
still get something.

NO DEAD AIR: something must happen every single second — an action, a reaction,
a sound, an effect, a change of expression. Two characters standing still doing
nothing is dead air, never comedy. Count the seconds and fill each one.

A HOLD IS ONE SECOND, NOT TWO: a pause only works when it lands right after a
fast burst and sits on ONE face that is visibly processing something. Anything
longer, or a pause on two motionless characters, is dead.

STACK SMALL GAGS: the main joke is not the only joke. Layer cheap visual gags
underneath it — paint splattered on his own face and not noticed, a handprint
left on his own belly, birds bursting out of the cave at a shout. Two or three
of these per clip cost nothing and fill every second.

DISMISSAL BEATS CONFRONTATION: a character who glances, grunts and walks
straight past is funnier than one who stops and stares. Reaction should be
under-sized, quick and mobile. Never park two characters in front of each other
to "react".

END ON A FACE, NOT A TABLEAU: close the clip on one character's expression
changing — a grin freezing, a tuft of hair drooping, eyes flicking to camera —
not on a wide shot of everyone standing there.
